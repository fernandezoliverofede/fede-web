Organizational Design & Effectiveness

Clarify how your organization is structured so strategy becomes executable — not something that gets stuck in the gap between plans and reality.

When structure gets in the way of strategy

Most organizational performance problems aren't people problems. They're design problems. When roles are unclear, decision-making is slow, or accountability is scattered, even the most capable teams struggle to perform.

Organizational design is the work of making your organization's structure, governance, and operating model fit what the business actually needs to achieve — not what made sense five years ago, or what the org chart inherited.

What this work covers

  • Organizational structure design and redesign — functions, teams, reporting lines, spans of control
  • Roles, responsibilities, and decision rights — who decides what, and how
  • Accountability frameworks and governance models
  • Operating model design — how work actually flows across the organization
  • Translating business strategy into organizational capability requirements
  • Post-merger or post-acquisition integration from an organizational perspective

How I approach it

Good organizational design starts with understanding the problem, not jumping to the solution. Before recommending a new structure, I take time to understand the business context, the current pain points, and — critically — why the existing design exists the way it does.

Most organizations have sensible explanations for their current structure. The question is whether that structure still serves the business it needs to support today.

From there, I work closely with leadership to develop structural options, evaluate trade-offs, and build the practical clarity needed to implement change without disrupting what's working.

Who this is for

This work is most relevant for organizations navigating growth, strategic pivots, mergers and acquisitions, or simply operating with structures that have outgrown the business they were designed for.

It tends to be most valuable when the CEO or a senior leader is directly involved — because organizational design decisions that don't have genuine leadership ownership rarely stick.
